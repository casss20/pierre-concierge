The Home Care Playbook

Straight answers for families caring for Mom or Dad at home

Hospitals discharge fast. Instructions arrive on thin paper. Medications change at every appointment. And the people doing the caring — usually a spouse or an adult child, often from out of state — are expected to just figure it out.

MODULE 1  The First 72 Hours Home

The most dangerous week of your parent's year is the week after discharge. About 1 in 5 seniors has a problem within a month of going home — and most of those problems were preventable. Here's how to set up right.

Before they leave the hospital — ask these 10 questions

  1. What changed? "Which of Mom's old medications were stopped, and which are new?" Get the answer in writing.
  2. What's the follow-up? Which doctor(s) do we see, and when? Book the appointments before discharge — not "later."
  3. What equipment do we need? Walker? Raised toilet seat? Oxygen? Ask for the order and the supplier phone number today.
  4. What are the warning signs? Ask directly: "What should worry me enough to call 911?" Write the answer down.
  5. Who do we call with questions? Get one phone number — not "your primary care doctor eventually."
  6. Can home health come? Ask whether Medicare home health (nurse, PT, OT) was ordered — and for how long.
  7. What about therapy? Which exercises, how often, and was a physical therapy referral sent?
  8. Diet & activity rules? Any restrictions on salt, fluids, stairs, driving, or lifting?
  9. Wound care? If there's an incision: how do we care for it, and when do staples/stitches come out?
  10. What paperwork comes home? Discharge summary, medication list, and follow-up orders — ask for copies of all three.

Stock the house before day one

  • All discharge medications picked up from the pharmacy that same day — ask the nurse to call them in early
  • Thermometer, blood pressure cuff, and a working scale if heart failure is in the picture
  • Clear walking paths; remove throw rugs; night lights from bed to bathroom; phone charger by the bed
  • Easy meals for 3 days, plenty of fluids, and a list of emergency contacts on the refrigerator
One thing to remember: the hospital gave you a plan for the hospital's worries. Your job in the first 72 hours is to build the plan for home's worries — meals, meds, movement, and who to call. That's what Module 2 is for.

MODULE 2  Ten Prescriptions, No System

The most common sentence I hear from families: "I can't remember if the 2 o'clock pill got taken." Here's the system that ends that sentence.

Step 1 — The One-Owner Rule

One person owns the medication system. Not "we all help." One name on the chart, one person refills, one person talks to the pharmacy. Shared responsibility = missed doses and double doses. Pick the owner today. If you're the out-of-town child, the owner is whoever is in the house — and you own the oversight calls.

Step 2 — Build the Master List

Fill in the table on page 7 (and keep the pharmacy's printout stapled behind it). Every pill must have: name, dose, what it's for, when it's taken, and who prescribed it. If nobody in the house can say what a pill is for — that pill needs a pharmacist or doctor review.

Step 3 — The Weekly Fill (Sundays)

  1. Line up all bottles, the printed list, and a 7-day organizer with AM/PM (or 4-time) compartments
  2. Fill one week at a time; check each pill against the list as you drop it in
  3. Check refills at the same sitting — call anything with fewer than 7 days left
  4. Sign the bottom of the page-7 log. That's your proof the week is set.

Step 4 — The Rules That Prevent Emergencies

  • Missed dose? Take it when remembered — never double the next dose to catch up. When in doubt, call the pharmacist (free, 24/7 at most chains).
  • New symptom after a new pill? Suspect the newest medication first. Note when it started and call the prescriber.
  • Brown-bag check every 6 months: every bottle, vitamin, and OTC pill goes in a bag to the pharmacist or doctor. Ask one question — "does Mom still need all of these?"
  • Insulin & blood thinners get a written schedule taped inside the cabinet door — these two cause the most home emergencies I see.
  • Nobody assumes. "She probably took it" is how hospitalizations begin. If it isn't logged, it didn't happen.
One thing to remember: the system protects you as much as it protects Mom. A missed-dose scare at 9 p.m. becomes a 30-second check, not a panicked phone call.

MODULE 3  The 2 A.M. Question: ER or Wait?

Every family caregiver knows this moment. It's late, something seems wrong, and you don't know if you're overreacting. Use this ladder. When truly in doubt — go. Nobody has ever regretted the ER visit that turned out to be nothing.

RED — Call 911 now. Do not wait, do not "see how the morning goes."
  • Chest pain, pressure, or pain spreading to arm/jaw · new trouble breathing
  • Face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech, sudden confusion (stroke signs — note the time it started)
  • Uncontrolled bleeding · a fall with head impact, especially on blood thinners
  • Signs of overdose or taking someone else's medication by mistake
  • Suicidal statements or severe agitation — call 988 for the crisis line if it's emotional, 911 if there's immediate danger
YELLOW — Call the doctor or nurse line first thing in the morning (or now if it's daytime).
  • Fever over 100.4°F that returns after Tylenol, or any fever with confusion
  • New redness, warmth, swelling, or drainage around a wound or incision
  • Eating/drinking poorly for 2+ days, new dizziness, or "just not herself" that persists
  • A missed dose of a blood thinner, insulin, seizure, or heart-rhythm medication
  • Two or more falls in a week, even without injury
GREEN — Manage at home and mention at the next appointment.
  • One-off poor appetite day · normal sleepiness after a busy day · a single stumble with a clear cause (loose shoe, dark hallway)
  • Mild constipation, dry skin, aches that respond to rest

MODULE 4  Talking to Doctors (and Getting Past the Privacy Wall)

The single most common — and most dangerous — problem I see: Dad's memory isn't what it was, the doctor explains things, Dad nods politely… and nobody actually knows what was said. Then the hospital calls you and, by law, can't tell you anything.

The three forms that remove the wall

  1. Release of Information (ROI) — one page, signed at each doctor's office and at the hospital. Names YOU as someone they may speak with. Free. Do it at the next visit, before you need it.
  2. Healthcare Power of Attorney / Surrogate — names who makes decisions if Dad can't. Florida hospitals have free forms; an elder-law attorney can do the full package (POA + healthcare surrogate + living will) for a few hundred dollars. Do this before any crisis — after capacity is lost, it's a court proceeding.
  3. The Appointment One-Pager — see page 7. A single sheet with conditions, meds, allergies, and your top three questions. Hand it to the nurse at check-in; suddenly everyone is on the same page.

The five questions that change every appointment

  1. "What are the top three things we should focus on between now and the next visit?"
  2. "Is any medication on this list no longer needed?" (Polypharmacy thrives on no one asking.)
  3. "What should make us call you before the next visit?"
  4. "Can we get test results explained in plain words — and copied to the family contact?"
  5. "What does 'worse' look like for this condition — what are we watching for?"
One thing to remember: doctors respond to organized families. Ten scattered questions get rushed answers. Three written questions on one page get real answers.

MODULE 5  The Family Meeting

One sibling does the work, another gives opinions from another state, a third disappears — and the primary caregiver burns out in silence. Sound familiar? Here's the meeting format that prevents it.

Call the meeting when…

…a parent is discharged from the hospital, has a new diagnosis, has two or more falls in six months, or before anyone is in crisis (the best time). 30–45 minutes, in person or by video. Everyone with a stake comes — including the out-of-town siblings.

The agenda (print this page)

  1. The medical picture (10 min) — the owner presents: diagnoses, meds count, appointments, red-flag status. One voice, one version of the truth.
  2. The tasks (15 min) — list every recurring task: meds, groceries, transport, bills, appointments. Assign each ONE owner and a backup. If a task has no owner, it belongs to the person who brought it up — or it goes on the "paid help" list.
  3. The money (10 min) — what does care cost now, what's coming, what can the parent afford, what can siblings contribute. Awkward for 10 minutes beats resentful for 10 years.
  4. The schedule (5 min) — when does the primary caregiver get a real break? Put it on the calendar like a doctor's appointment. Respite isn't optional; it's maintenance.

Ground rules that keep it civil

  • Talk about what Mom/Dad needs — not about what happened at Christmas 1998
  • No "you never / you always." Say "I need" instead
  • One person speaks at a time; the organizer enforces it kindly
  • Out-of-state siblings: jobs are information-gathering, bill-paying, and scheduled visits — presence is a task too
  • End with the shared care log (page 7) agreed on by everyone
One thing to remember: siblings fight about care when the plan is invisible. Write it down, share it, and arguments turn into logistics.
