The Holiday Parent Checkup

You're home for a few days. Here's how to really see how Mom or Dad is doing —
and what to do if what you see worries you.
Pierre Concierge Nursing · Elda Marie Joe Pierre, RN, BSN · Ocala & Marion County, FL

Every November and December, adult children fly home and notice it: the fridge is empty in a new way, Dad's lost weight, there are dents in the car. You've got one short visit to see how they're really doing. This guide turns it into a structured assessment — the same categories a nurse checks first. Work through it casually, over normal moments. Don't interrogate — observe, note, check boxes. About 20 minutes total.

1. Appearance & weight — when you hug them hello

 What to check — and what it can mean
☐Clothes fit the same as last visit? Loose rings, cinched belts, baggy clothes = weight loss — a worry flag.
☐Skin and eyes look healthy — not gray, sunken, or dehydrated. Dehydration drives most senior ER visits.
☐Steady standing up from a low chair — watch the push-off. Both arms pushing off = new weakness.
☐Personal hygiene unchanged — hair, nails, teeth, grooming. A sudden slide is usually cognition, not vanity.

2. The kitchen & the pills — offer to make coffee

 What to check — and what it can mean
☐Fridge has fresh, varied food — not just condiments and old leftovers. A "food desert" fridge = shopping, transport, or money trouble.
☐Dishes done, trash out, no smell of spoiled food or scorched pans. Scorched pans = forgetting the stove — check smoke detectors today.
☐Pill organizer is current — today's compartment is what's open, not a jumble. A jumble = the system broke down weeks ago.
☐New pill bottles from new doctors — count them against last year. More doctors = more conflicting advice, nobody coordinating.
☐Any unopened prescriptions? The most common post-hospital failure I see.

3. Movement & falls — walk with them, don't watch from the couch

 What to check — and what it can mean
☐Rises from a low chair without using both arms to push. New two-arm push-off = leg weakness.
☐Stairs handled confidently — no new white-knuckle grip on the rail. New stair fear = a fall they've hidden from you.
☐Bathroom set up: grab bars, non-slip mat, night light on the bed-to-bath path. Most falls happen at night, on that exact path.
☐Ask directly: "Any falls this year? Even one?" Most seniors don't report falls. One is common; two or more in six months is a pattern that needs a plan.

4. Car & driving — ask them to drive you somewhere

 What to check — and what it can mean
☐New dents, scrapes, or a side mirror held on with tape. The car tells the truth the parent won't.
☐Misses turns on familiar routes, confuses pedals, crawls far under traffic speed. These patterns mean it's time for a formal driving evaluation.
☐Ask a sibling or neighbor casually: has anyone stopped riding with them? Families whisper this tell — collect it gently.
One thing to remember: you're not looking for perfection — you're looking for change from last visit. The direction matters more than any single snapshot.

5. Mind & mood — over a meal, the longest unhurried conversation you'll get

 What to check — and what it can mean
☐Conversation flows — they follow stories, remember names, finish thoughts. Repeating the same story three times in one evening is a flag.
☐They ask you questions back. Interest in your life is a strong cognition signal.
☐Ask about their week: specifics, or vague "same as always"? Vagueness that shields decline is common.
☐Unopened mail stacked up? Late notices, unpaid bills? Money confusion is often the first sign — before memory.
☐Mood: holiday sadness is normal; withdrawal and "what's the point" talk that persists is not. Persistent low mood deserves a doctor mention — and 988 if it ever sounds dangerous.

6. The conversation starters — pick the moment: a drive, a dish-drying session

 Say this — and why it works
☐"What has been the hardest part of the week?" Open-ended — listen for what's NOT mentioned: friends, outings, church.
☐"If you needed help with something, who would you call?" Tests their real support network, not the imagined one.
☐"Have you two talked about what happens if one of you can't manage alone?" The door-opener for healthcare surrogate, POA, and care planning. Awkward for 30 seconds; priceless for the next decade.

What your checklist is telling you

GREEN — mostly checked, nothing alarming: enjoy the holiday. Do the "before you leave" setup below, and schedule a real check-in for spring.
YELLOW — a few unchecked boxes, or one worry flag: book a professional wellness assessment within 30 days, get the medication list reconciled, and set up weekly calls with a script.
RED — weight loss + falls + med chaos + money confusion, or any sudden change: that's not aging, that's a problem. Get a nurse or geriatrician's eyes on them this month. In Ocala/Marion County, this is exactly what I do — often within 24–48 hours.

Before you fly home — the 30-minute setup

  • Fill in the refrigerator emergency card (in The Home Care Playbook) and photograph it
  • Photograph every medication bottle + the insurance card — full label, both sides
  • Save their doctor's, pharmacy's, and one neighbor's number in your phone
  • Ask them to sign a Release of Information at their next appointment so doctors can talk to you
  • Agree on a weekly call time — and who notices if they don't answer
  • If anything worried you today: book the assessment before you leave the house. "Later" is where this goes to die.
