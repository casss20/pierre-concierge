The Holiday Parent Checkup

You're home for a few days. Here's how to really see how Mom or Dad is doing —
and what to do if what you see worries you.

Every November and December, adult children fly home and notice it: the fridge is empty in a new way, Dad's lost weight, there are dents in the car, pills are scattered on the counter. You've got one short visit to figure out how they're really doing. This guide turns that visit into a structured assessment — the same categories a nurse checks first.

Work through it casually, over the visit. Don't interrogate — observe, note, and check boxes. It takes about 20 minutes total, spread across normal moments.

1️⃣ Appearance & weight (when you hug them hello)

Clothes fit the same as last visit? Loose rings, belts cinched tight, or baggy clothes = worry

Skin and eyes look healthy — not gray, sunken, or dehydrated

They're steady on their feet when they stand up from a chair (watch the push-off with both arms — that's a tell)

Personal hygiene unchanged — hair, nails, teeth, grooming

2️⃣ The kitchen & the pills (offer to make coffee)

Fridge has fresh, varied food — not just condiments and old leftovers

Dishes done, trash out, no smell of spoiled food or scorched pans

Pill organizer is current — today's compartment is what's open, not a jumble

Look for new pill bottles from new doctors. Count them against what you knew about last year — more doctors = more conflicting advice

Any unopened prescriptions? That's the most common post-hospital failure I see.

3️⃣ Movement & falls (walk with them, don't watch from the couch)

They rise from a low chair without using both arms to push

Stairs handled confidently — no white-knuckle rail grip or one-step-at-a-time caution that's new

Check the bathroom: grab bars? Non-slip mat? Night light on the path from bed?

Ask directly: "Any falls this year? Even one?" Most seniors don't report falls. One fall is common; two or more in six months is a pattern that needs a plan.

4️⃣ Car & driving (ask them to drive you somewhere — the grocery store counts)

New dents, scrapes, or a side mirror held on with tape

They miss turns on familiar routes, confuse pedals, or drive noticeably under the speed of traffic

Passengers or neighbors have stopped riding with them (ask a sibling or neighbor casually — this is the tell families whisper about)

One thing to remember: you're not looking for perfection. You're looking for change from last visit. Decline between visits is the signal; the direction matters more than any single snapshot.

5️⃣ Mind & mood (over a meal — the longest unhurried conversation you'll get)

Conversation flows — they follow stories, remember the names you're talking about, finish thoughts

They ask you questions back — interest in your life is a strong cognition signal

Ask about their week: do their answers have specifics, or vague "oh, same as always"?

Unopened mail stacked up? Missed bills, late notices, or renewed subscriptions to things they don't remember buying — money confusion is often the first sign, before memory

Mood: some holiday sadness is normal. Withdrawal, stopped hobbies, or "what's the point" talk that persists is different — mention it to their doctor

6️⃣ The conversation starters (pick the moment — a drive, a dish-drying session)

These three questions open more doors than any interrogation:

"What has been the hardest part of the week?" — open-ended; listen for what's NOT mentioned (friends, outings, church)

"If you needed help with something, who would you call?" — tests their real support network, not the imagined one

"Have you and Dad/Mom talked about what happens if one of you can't manage alone?" — the door-opener for healthcare surrogate, POA, and care planning conversations. Awkward for 30 seconds; priceless for the next decade

What your checklist is telling you

Mostly checked, nothing alarming — enjoy the holiday. Do the "before you leave" setup below, and schedule a real check-in for spring.
A few unchecked boxes, or one "worry" flag — don't panic, but don't fly home pretending you didn't see it. Book a professional wellness assessment in the next 30 days, get their medication list reconciled, and set up weekly calls with a script (see my Long-Distance Caregiver Kit).
Weight loss + falls + med chaos + money confusion, or any sudden change — that's not aging, that's a problem. Get a nurse or geriatrician eyes on them this month. If they're in Ocala/Marion County, this is exactly what I do — often within 24–48 hours.

Before you fly home — the 30-minute setup

Fill in the refrigerator emergency card (in The Home Care Playbook) and photograph it

Photograph every medication bottle + the insurance card — full label, both sides

Save their doctor's, pharmacy's, and one neighbor's number in your phone

Ask them to sign a Release of Information at their next appointment so doctors can talk to you

Agree on a weekly call time — and who notices if they don't answer

If anything worried you today: book the assessment before you leave the house. "Later" is where this goes to die.
